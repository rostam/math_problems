Problem 2 · Number theory

Goldbach's Conjecture

Is every even number greater than 2 the sum of two primes? For example, 28 = 5 + 23.

Statement. Every even integer greater than 2 can be written as the sum of two primes.
First posed
Goldbach's letter to Euler, 1742
Verified up to
4 × 10¹⁸
Weak version
Proven (Helfgott, 2013)

Why it matters

It is one of the oldest and best-known unsolved problems in all of mathematics. It is a pure test of how primes interact with addition. Primes are defined by multiplication, so questions mixing them with addition tend to be very hard.

The attempts have produced lasting tools: the Hardy–Littlewood circle method, Vinogradov's estimates for exponential sums, and much of modern sieve theory.

Where things stand

Open. The weak (ternary) Goldbach conjecture says every odd number greater than 5 is a sum of three primes. Harald Helfgott proved it completely in 2013.

For the original (binary) version, we know that almost all even numbers are sums of two primes: any exceptions are extremely rare. We also know every large enough even number is a prime plus a number with at most two prime factors (Chen).

The number of ways to write 2n as a sum of two primes appears to grow steadily with n. A plot of these counts, called the "Goldbach comet", makes it very believable that the count never drops to zero.

History of attempts

  • 1742Christian Goldbach writes to Euler proposing the conjecture.
  • 1923Hardy and Littlewood develop the circle method and give a conditional attack on the ternary problem.
  • 1930Schnirelmann proves every integer greater than 1 is a sum of at most some fixed number of primes.
  • 1937Vinogradov proves the weak conjecture for all sufficiently large odd numbers.
  • 1938Chudakov, van der Corput and Estermann show almost all even numbers are sums of two primes.
  • 1973Chen: every large even number is prime + (prime or semiprime).
  • 2013Helfgott completes the proof of the weak Goldbach conjecture. Oliveira e Silva, Herzog and Pardi verify the binary one up to 4 × 10¹⁸.

Ideas for solving it

  • Circle method. This method counts representations by integrating an exponential sum around a circle. For three primes, the "minor arcs" (the messy part) can be controlled. For two primes they cannot. Any new way to bound that error term would be a breakthrough.
  • Sieve plus parity. As with twin primes, sieves stall at "prime plus almost-prime" because of the parity problem. Ideas that break parity for one problem often transfer to the other.
  • Shrink the exceptional set. Proving that the number of exceptions up to x is at most xδ for ever smaller δ is steady, measurable progress.

Try it yourself

For each even number from 4 to 100, count how many ways it can be written as a sum of two primes. Plot the counts. You have drawn the start of the Goldbach comet. Which numbers have unusually many representations? (Hint: look at multiples of 3 and 5.)
