Problem 11 · Geometry and combinatorics

Union-Closed Sets Conjecture (Frankl)

Take a family of sets where the union of any two sets in the family is also in it. Is some element contained in at least half of the sets?

Statement. If a finite family of sets (not just the empty set) is closed under unions, then some element belongs to at least half of the sets in the family.
First posed
Péter Frankl, 1979
Best known fraction
just above 38.2%
Target
50%

Why it matters

It is one of the most famous "simple" problems in extremal combinatorics. It is also equivalent to natural statements about lattices and about graphs.

The 2022 breakthrough is a textbook example of an idea from another field, information theory (entropy), suddenly unlocking a decades-old problem.

Where things stand

Open. Before 2022, nobody could even show that some element lies in a fixed positive fraction of the sets.

In late 2022, Justin Gilmer (then at Google) proved a fraction of 1%. Within days, several groups improved it to (3 − √5)/2 ≈ 38.2%: Alweiss–Huang–Sellke, Chase–Lovett, Sawin, and others. That number is a natural limit of the entropy method, and only tiny improvements beyond it are known.

The conjecture is verified for families on small ground sets and for many special classes.

History of attempts

  • 1979Frankl formulates the conjecture.
  • 1990s–2010sMany special cases are proven: small ground sets, families with few sets, lattice formulations and graph versions.
  • 2022Gilmer's entropy argument gives the first constant fraction (1%).
  • 2022Several groups sharpen it to ≈ 38.2% within weeks, with small improvements beyond.

Ideas for solving it

  • Refine the entropy method. Gilmer compares two random sets from the family and bounds the entropy of their union. Pushing past 38.2% seems to need new coupling or correlation ideas.
  • Structural reductions. Show that a minimal counterexample must have special structure, then rule that structure out.
  • Lattice and graph versions. The equivalent graph formulation (about maximal stable sets in bipartite graphs) may be easier to attack with graph-theoretic tools.

Try it yourself

Take the family {∅, {1}, {2,3}, {1,2,3}} and check that it is closed under unions. Count how many sets contain each element. Now try to build a union-closed family where every element appears in fewer than half the sets. Why does it always fail?
