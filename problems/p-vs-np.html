Problem 7 · Computer science and logic

P vs NP

If a solution is quick to check, is it also quick to find? Sudoku is an example: checking a filled grid is easy, but solving one seems hard.

Statement. Is every problem whose solutions can be verified in polynomial time (NP) also solvable in polynomial time (P)? Most researchers believe P ≠ NP.
Formalized
Cook (1971) and Levin (1973)
Prize

Why it matters

If P = NP with a practical algorithm, most public-key cryptography would break. Many hard optimization problems in logistics, chip design and biology would become easy. Even finding mathematical proofs would become roughly as easy as checking them.

If P ≠ NP, as expected, it would confirm that creativity and search are fundamentally harder than verification. It would also put the foundations of cryptography on firm ground.

Thousands of natural problems are NP-complete: Sudoku, scheduling, the traveling salesperson problem, protein folding models, Tetris and more. A fast algorithm for any one of them would solve all of them.

Where things stand

Open, and widely considered one of the hardest problems in mathematics. Three proven "barriers" show that whole families of proof techniques cannot resolve it on their own: relativization, natural proofs and algebrization.

Researchers can prove strong lower bounds for restricted models of computation, such as constant-depth circuits and monotone circuits. Extending these to general circuits is the central challenge.

History of attempts

  • 1956Gödel asks von Neumann, in a letter, essentially this question.
  • 1971Cook proves that satisfiability (SAT) is NP-complete. Levin finds the same result independently in the USSR.
  • 1972Karp shows 21 classic problems are NP-complete.
  • 1975Baker, Gill and Solovay: the relativization barrier.
  • 1981–87Furst–Saxe–Sipser, Ajtai, Håstad: parity cannot be computed by small constant-depth circuits. Razborov proves exponential lower bounds for monotone circuits.
  • 1994Razborov and Rudich: the natural proofs barrier.
  • 2008Aaronson and Wigderson: the algebrization barrier.
  • 2011Ryan Williams proves NEXP ⊄ ACC⁰ by turning faster algorithms into lower bounds.

Ideas for solving it

  • Circuit lower bounds. Show that some NP problem requires super-polynomial circuit size. Progress here has been slow but real.
  • Algorithms to lower bounds. Williams's program shows that even slightly faster-than-brute-force algorithms for circuit problems imply new lower bounds. It is one of the few approaches known to get around the barriers.
  • Geometric Complexity Theory. Mulmuley and Sohoni use algebraic geometry and representation theory to separate algebraic analogues of P and NP (permanent vs determinant).
  • Proof complexity. Show that certain proof systems need super-polynomially long proofs of some tautologies. Doing this for every proof system would prove NP ≠ coNP, and hence P ≠ NP.

Try it yourself

Solve a hard Sudoku and time yourself. Then have a friend check your solution and time them. The gap between those two times is the intuition behind P vs NP.
