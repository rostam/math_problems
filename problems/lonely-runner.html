Problem 12 · Geometry and combinatorics

Lonely Runner Conjecture

n runners start together on a circular track of length 1, each at a different constant speed. Does every runner at some moment end up at least 1/n of a lap away from all the others?

Statement. If n runners start together on a circular track of length 1 and run forever at distinct constant speeds, then each runner is at some time at distance at least 1/n from every other runner.
First posed
Wills (1967), Cusick (1973)
Proven for
up to 7 runners (2008)
Bound 1/n is
sharp (speeds 1, 2, …, n)

Why it matters

It is a problem in Diophantine approximation: how well can multiples of several real numbers avoid the integers at the same time?

It has many equivalent forms. Cusick came to it through "view obstruction" (seeing out of a forest of cubes). It is also connected to flows in graphs and to coloring certain distance graphs.

Where things stand

Open in general. Proven for up to 7 runners (Barajas and Serra, 2008). Recent computer-assisted work has been reported to push this further.

Tao showed in 2018 that, to check the conjecture for a given number of runners, it is enough to check speeds below some explicit bound. So each fixed case is in principle a finite computation, though the bound is enormous.

The bound 1/n cannot be improved: with speeds 0, 1, 2, …, n−1, the runner at speed 0 is never farther than 1/n from everyone.

History of attempts

  • 1967Wills poses the problem in the language of Diophantine approximation.
  • 1973Cusick independently arrives at it through view obstruction.
  • 1998Bienia et al. give the "lonely runner" name and link it to nowhere-zero flows.
  • 2001Bohman, Holzman and Kleitman prove it for 6 runners (with a simpler proof by Renault in 2004).
  • 2008Barajas and Serra prove it for 7 runners.
  • 2018Tao reduces each case to finitely many speed sets.

Ideas for solving it

  • Finite checks plus computers. Shrink Tao's speed bound until each next case can be checked by computer.
  • Zonotope geometry. Henze and Malikiosis reformulated it in terms of covering radii of zonotopes (sums of line segments). This brings in tools from convex geometry.
  • Relaxations. Prove the conjecture with a slightly weaker distance, say 1/(2n), and then tighten. Also consider "almost all speeds" versions, which are known.

Try it yourself

Simulate 3 runners with speeds 1, 2 and 3 on a track of length 1 in a spreadsheet. Record each runner's distance to the nearest other runner at time steps of 0.01. Does each one ever get 1/3 of a lap away from both others?
