Problem 18 · Graph theory

Erdős–Gyárfás Conjecture

Suppose every vertex in a graph has at least 3 neighbors. Must the graph contain a cycle whose length is a power of 2 (4, 8, 16, …)?

Statement. Every graph in which every vertex has degree at least 3 contains a cycle whose length is a power of 2.
First posed
Erdős and Gyárfás, 1995
Smallest counterexample
would need at least 17 vertices
Large-degree version
Proven (Liu–Montgomery)

Why it matters

It is about the cycle spectrum: which cycle lengths a graph must contain. Minimum degree 3 forces many cycles, and the question is whether they can all dodge the powers of 2, which get sparser as they grow.

Erdős offered money for it: $100 for a proof and $50 for a counterexample. This reflects his belief that it is true but that a counterexample wouldn't be shocking.

Where things stand

Open. Known for several classes, including claw-free graphs and 3-connected cubic planar graphs (Heckman and Krakovski, 2013).

Computer searches show any counterexample must have at least 17 vertices, and a cubic one at least 30.

Liu and Montgomery proved that if the minimum (or average) degree is large enough, the graph must contain a cycle of power-of-2 length. The difficulty is the small value 3.

History of attempts

  • 1995Erdős and Gyárfás pose the conjecture.
  • 2000sProven for claw-free graphs and several other classes. Computer searches establish lower bounds on counterexamples.
  • 2013Heckman and Krakovski prove it for 3-connected cubic planar graphs.
  • 2020–23Liu and Montgomery prove the large-degree version with sublinear expanders.

Ideas for solving it

  • Lower the degree threshold. Liu and Montgomery's expander methods need a large constant. Reducing it toward 3 would be decisive.
  • Cubic graphs first. A minimal counterexample can be assumed to be nearly cubic, which is a very structured setting for case analysis and computer search.
  • Cycle-length arithmetic. Results showing that graphs contain cycles of many consecutive lengths, or lengths in many residue classes, might be sharpened to hit a power of 2.

Try it yourself

Draw the Petersen graph (every vertex has degree 3) and find a cycle of length 8. Then try the complete bipartite graph K₃,₃, which has cycles of length 4 immediately. Can you design a degree-3 graph whose shortest cycle has length 5 and that avoids length 8?
