Problem 19 · Graph theory

Seymour's Second Neighborhood Conjecture

Imagine a social network where "follows" goes one way and nobody follows each other back. Is there always someone whose followers-of-followers (people two steps away who aren't already direct followers) are at least as many as their direct followers?

Statement. In every oriented graph (a directed graph with no 2-cycles), some vertex v has at least as many vertices at distance exactly 2 as at distance 1: |N⁺⁺(v)| ≥ |N⁺(v)|.
First posed
Paul Seymour, 1990
Proven for
Tournaments (1996)
Best general ratio
≈ 0.657 (and a bit more)

Why it matters

It captures a basic intuition about influence and spread: in a network without mutual links, someone's reach roughly doubles at the second step.

It implies a special case of the Caccetta–Häggkvist conjecture on short directed cycles, another major open problem.

Where things stand

Open. Proven for tournaments, where every pair of vertices has exactly one arc between them (Fisher, 1996; a simpler proof by Havet and Thomassé, 2000, using "median orders"). Also proven for several other dense or structured classes.

In general, Chen, Shen and Yuster (2003) showed some vertex has |N⁺⁺| ≥ γ|N⁺| with γ ≈ 0.657, and there have been small improvements since.

History of attempts

  • 1990Seymour poses the conjecture.
  • 1996Fisher proves it for tournaments (Dean's conjecture) using probability.
  • 2000Havet and Thomassé give a combinatorial proof for tournaments via median orders.
  • 2003Chen, Shen and Yuster prove the γ ≈ 0.657 bound for all oriented graphs.
  • 2010sProven for further classes: dense graphs, graphs with small maximum degree, and others.

Ideas for solving it

  • Median orders. Extend the Havet–Thomassé technique from tournaments to graphs with some missing arcs. There has been partial success when few arcs are missing.
  • Push the constant. Improve γ from about 0.66 toward 1 with sharper counting or weighting arguments.
  • Minimal counterexamples. Show a smallest counterexample has no "sink-like" vertices and strong regularity, then derive a contradiction.

Try it yourself

Draw 5 people in a circle, each following the next two clockwise. Each person has 2 direct follows and 2 second-step follows, so equality holds. Now remove one arrow and check whether the property still holds.
