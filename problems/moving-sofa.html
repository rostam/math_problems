Problem 9 · Geometry and combinatorics

Moving Sofa Problem

What is the largest shape you can carry around a right-angled corner in a hallway 1 unit wide?

Statement. Find the rigid planar shape of largest area that can be moved around a right-angled corner in a hallway of width 1.
First posed
Leo Moser, 1966
Best known sofa
Gerver's, area ≈ 2.2195
Claimed proof
Jineon Baek, 2024

Why it matters

It is a clean example of shape optimization, where the unknown is an entire shape rather than a number. Problems like this are notoriously hard because the space of possible shapes is infinite-dimensional.

It has become a test case for combining geometric insight with computer-assisted proofs.

Where things stand

Possibly solved. In 2024 Jineon Baek posted a proof, over 100 pages long, that Gerver's sofa is optimal. Experts are still checking it. Before that, the best upper bound was about 2.37 (Kallus and Romik, 2018).

Gerver's sofa is bounded by 18 distinct curve pieces. The claimed answer, about 2.2195, has no known simple closed form.

History of attempts

  • 1966Leo Moser poses the problem.
  • 1968Hammersley finds a telephone-shaped sofa of area π/2 + 2/π ≈ 2.2074, and proves no sofa can exceed 2√2 ≈ 2.83.
  • 1992Gerver finds a slightly better sofa (≈ 2.2195) and conjectures it is optimal.
  • 2018Kallus and Romik use computer-assisted proofs to show no sofa exceeds 2.37. Romik also finds exact formulas for Gerver's sofa and solves an "ambidextrous" variant.
  • 2024Baek claims a full proof that Gerver's sofa is optimal.

Ideas for solving it

  • Verify Baek's proof. The most important current task is careful checking and, ideally, simplification.
  • Convex relaxation. Baek's approach reformulates the problem so that an upper bound becomes a concave optimization, which can be solved exactly. Finding simpler relaxations could give a shorter proof.
  • Variants. Other corridor angles, three-dimensional versions and ambidextrous sofas are open and may be more approachable.

Try it yourself

Cut shapes out of paper and push them around an L-shaped corridor drawn with 1-unit width (say 5 cm). A semicircle of radius 1 (area ≈ 1.57) works. Can you beat Hammersley's 2.2074 by hand?
