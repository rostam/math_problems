Problem 6 · Number theory

Legendre's Conjecture

Is there always a prime between n² and (n+1)²?

Statement. For every positive integer n, there is a prime between n² and (n + 1)².
First posed
Adrien-Marie Legendre, c. 1800
Landau's problems
One of Landau's four (1912)
Best known
Primes in [x, x + x0.525]

Why it matters

It is a statement about primes in short intervals. The interval from n² to (n+1)² has length about 2√x when x = n². So the conjecture says that gaps between primes are always shorter than about 2√x.

Remarkably, even the Riemann Hypothesis doesn't imply it: RH gives gaps of size √x · ln x, which is just barely too weak. That makes it a good measure of how far beyond RH our understanding of primes has to go.

Where things stand

Open. The best unconditional result guarantees a prime in every interval [x, x + x0.525] for large x (Baker, Harman and Pintz, 2001). Legendre needs exponent 1/2.

Ingham proved the cubic analogue in 1937: there is always a prime between n³ and (n+1)³ for large n. Later work made "large" explicit.

History of attempts

  • 1845–52Bertrand conjectures, and Chebyshev proves, that there is always a prime between n and 2n.
  • 1912Landau lists it among four "unattackable" problems about primes (the other three: Goldbach, twin primes, and primes of the form n² + 1).
  • 1936Cramér conjectures prime gaps are at most about (ln x)². This would imply Legendre easily.
  • 1937Ingham: primes between consecutive cubes (for large n).
  • 2001Baker, Harman and Pintz: a prime in [x, x + x0.525].

Ideas for solving it

  • Lower the exponent. Each improvement from 0.525 toward 0.5 comes from sharper zero-density estimates and sieve refinements. The recent Guth–Maynard zero-density bound improves results about primes in short intervals "on average".
  • Go beyond RH. Since RH alone is insufficient, a proof needs additional input, such as the pair-correlation conjecture or good control of zeros near the critical line.
  • Averaged versions. Proving it holds for almost all n is already within reach of current methods. Upgrading "almost all" to "all" is the hard part.

Try it yourself

For n = 1 to 30, count the primes between n² and (n+1)². The minimum count stays at 1 or 2 for small n but trends upward. Does it look like it could ever hit 0?
