Problem 20 · Graph theory

Lovász Conjecture

Call a graph "symmetric" if every vertex looks exactly like every other vertex. Does every connected symmetric graph have a path that visits each vertex exactly once?

Statement. Every finite connected vertex-transitive graph has a Hamiltonian path.
First posed
László Lovász, 1969
Known graphs without a Hamiltonian cycle
Only 5
Cayley graph version
Also open

Why it matters

It links symmetry (group theory) and traversal (Hamiltonicity). Finding Hamiltonian paths is NP-hard in general, so the conjecture says symmetry makes an otherwise hard problem always solvable.

For Cayley graphs of groups, a Hamiltonian cycle lists every element of the group, moving by one generator at a time. Gray codes, which list binary strings changing one bit at a time, are the hypercube case.

Where things stand

Open. Only five connected vertex-transitive graphs are known that lack a Hamiltonian cycle: K₂, the Petersen graph, the Coxeter graph, and the two graphs obtained from Petersen and Coxeter by replacing each vertex with a triangle. All five still have Hamiltonian paths.

For Cayley graphs, a special kind of vertex-transitive graph, the stronger conjecture is that every connected one on 3 or more vertices has a Hamiltonian cycle. This is proven for abelian groups, for groups of prime-power order (Witte, 1986), and for many groups of special orders.

Dense vertex-transitive graphs are Hamiltonian (Christofides, Hladký and Máthé, 2014). Some experts, notably Babai, suspect the general conjecture may be false.

History of attempts

  • 1953Gray codes (patented by Frank Gray) give Hamiltonian cycles on hypercubes.
  • 1969Lovász poses the problem, originally phrased as a challenge to find a counterexample.
  • 1986Witte: Cayley graphs of p-groups have Hamiltonian cycles.
  • 2014Christofides, Hladký and Máthé: dense vertex-transitive graphs are Hamiltonian.

Ideas for solving it

  • Factor group lemma. Find a Hamiltonian cycle in a quotient graph and "lift" it to the full Cayley graph. This is the main tool in the Cayley case.
  • Classify by order. Prove it for all vertex-transitive graphs whose number of vertices has a given form (p, 2p, p2, pq, …) and expand the list.
  • Search for counterexamples. Computer searches over small vertex-transitive graphs continue. A sixth non-Hamiltonian example would be big news.

Try it yourself

Draw the 3-dimensional cube (8 vertices, labeled with binary strings 000 to 111). Find a closed tour that changes one bit at a time: 000, 001, 011, 010, 110, 111, 101, 100. That's a Gray code. Then find a path through all 10 vertices of the Petersen graph. It exists, but no matter how you try, you can't close it into a cycle.
